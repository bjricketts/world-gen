## 1. Suitability and population density

**Methods.**
- Population density is modelled as a function of physical covariates. Nordhaus (2006, G-Econ) regresses output and population on 1° cells against climate, distance to coast, elevation and soils; Beck & Sieber (2010) predict the four subsistence modes (agriculture, animal husbandry, pastoralism, hunting and gathering) from climate and soil alone with ecological niche models. Both show that physical geography explains most of the large-scale pattern.
- The main covariates and their Earth relations: distance to coast and elevation (Cohen & Small 1998; Small & Cohen 2004), distance to fresh water (Kummu et al. 2011), mean annual temperature (the human climate niche of Xu et al. 2020), cultivable land from climate and soil (Ramankutty et al. 2002).
- Pre-agricultural density follows net primary productivity, with biodiversity and pathogen stress as secondary terms (Tallavaara et al. 2018); Kavanagh et al. (2018) hindcast hunter-gatherer densities from environment and find that agriculture arose where those densities were high.
- Historical population by cell: HYDE 3.2 (Klein Goldewijk et al. 2017) gives population, cropland and pasture per 5′ cell for 10 000 BCE–2017 CE, so density-by-development can be read at matching epochs.

**Fit to the model.** Every covariate already exists on the surface: `productivity`, `koppen`, `biome`, `air_temperature`, `precipitation`, `discharge`, `lake`, `river_order`, `ocean`, `elevation`, and landform slope. Suitability is a product of factors in [0, 1], with the weights set by development level (NPP for pre-agricultural, cultivable land for agrarian, resources and coast for industrial and later).

**Validation.** Numbers taken from the papers (task 8.3):
- Elevation (Cohen & Small 1998, 1994 population on 19 032 administrative units averaging ~7000 km²): 33.5% of people live below 100 m, on 15.6% of occupied land; the median person lives at 194 m, 75% below 523 m. Density quantiles: the median person lives at 262 people/km² and 75% at or below 648; the median km² of occupied land holds 4.3 and 75% of it 24.5 or less. Occupied land is 129.7 million km², 97.5% of ice-free land.
- Coast (Small & Cohen 2004, GPW2): 38, 44, 50 and 67% of people live within 100, 150, 200 and 400 km of a coastline; half the population occupies less than 3% of the land (2.5′ cells).
- Fresh water (Kummu et al. 2011, 1 km grid, lakes and three river classes): over 50% of people live within 3 km of surface fresh water and 10% beyond 10 km; median distance 3.0 km (2.2 to large rivers, 4.6 to lakes). The ratio of the population's median distance to the land's is 0.88 in the median region, so distance to water shapes population mainly in arid zones. These distances are below the global grid spacing and become a zoom-level check.
- Temperature niche (Xu et al. 2020): main mode of population at 11–15 °C mean annual temperature, secondary mode at 20–25 °C (the Indian monsoon region); mean annual temperatures above 29 °C cover 0.8% of land.
- Foragers (Tallavaara et al. 2018, 300 ethnographic groups): ln density (people per 100 km²) rises 0.003 per g m⁻² yr⁻¹ of climatic (Miami-model) NPP up to a breakpoint at ~1360, then levels off, where pathogen stress takes over as the limit; observed densities span roughly ln −1 to 5 per 100 km² (0.004–1.5 per km²). Highest predicted densities are in temperate oceanic forests, then subtropical humid forests.
- World population (HYDE 3.2, Klein Goldewijk et al. 2017): 4.4 million in 10 000 BCE, 19 million in 5000 BCE, 232 million in 1 CE, 323 million in 1000, 592 million in 1700, 943 million in 1800, 1.64 billion in 1900, 2.53 billion in 1950, 6.11 billion in 2000 and 7.26 billion in 2015.

## 2. Resources from the tectonic setting

**Methods.** Deposit types follow geodynamic setting (Groves & Bierlein 2007):
- porphyry copper (and Mo, Au) in magmatic arcs above subduction zones (Sillitoe 2010; world database, Singer et al. 2008);
- orogenic gold in accretionary and collisional belts, largely Archean, Paleoproterozoic and Phanerozoic orogens (Groves et al. 1998; Goldfarb et al. 2001);
- iron formations on old cratonic crust (Bekker et al. 2010);
- hydrocarbons in sedimentary basins: of 877 giant fields, 35% lie on passive margins, 31% in rifts and sags, 20% in collisional forelands, 8% in accreted terranes and arc collisions, 6% on strike-slip margins and under 1% on subduction margins (Mann et al. 2003);
- coal where a vegetated, wet, subsiding basin buries plant matter; the Carboniferous coal peak was not caused by delayed fungal evolution (Nelsen et al. 2016), which leaves basin subsidence and wet climate as the controls;
- evaporites in arid closed or restricted basins (Warren 2010).

**Fit to the model.** The surface stores plate boundary type, crust type, crust age, orogeny age, landforms (arcs, rifts, collision belts, shelves), sea-floor age, endorheic basins, vegetation and climate. Each deposit type becomes a potential field from these, with deterministic noise for individual deposits.

**Validation.** Shares of generated hydrocarbon potential by setting against Mann et al. (2003); porphyry potential confined to arcs; iron formations only on crust older than ~1.8 Gyr where the planet's history allows it.

**Result (task 8.2).** Fifteen classes were built (the six extra: tin–tungsten in collision belts, Romer & Kroner 2016; uranium in basins after oxygenation and in collision granites, Cuney 2009; chromite and platinum metals in ophiolites, González-Jiménez et al. 2014, and intraplate intrusions, Maier & Groves 2011; rare earths in rifts and tropical clays, Smith et al. 2016; geothermal heat, Bertani 2016; placer gold, heuristic). The simulation had almost no continental sediment, so subsidence (McKenzie 1978; DeCelles & Giles 1996) and biogenic sediment (Milliman 1993; Bosscher & Schlager 1992; Schlager 2003; Opdyke & Wilkinson 1988) were added and checked against CRUST1.0 (Laske et al. 2013) and GlobSed (Straume et al. 2019): continental crust with >1 km of sediment rose from 1–3% to 23–30% (Earth 38%). Hydrocarbon setting factors were then fitted to Mann et al. (2003), so those shares are a calibration, not a test. Details in DESIGN.md 4.5, 4.8, 5.1 and 7.

## 3. Settlement sizes

**Methods and data.**
- Rank-size: Zipf's law with exponent near 1, explained by Gibrat growth (Gabaix 1999). Across 73 countries Soo (2005) finds exponents mostly above 1 with the Clauset et al. (2009) caveat that power-law fits need maximum-likelihood tests. Rozenfeld et al. (2010) show that city clusters built from settlement footprints follow Zipf closely, and Rozenfeld et al. (2008) study the growth statistics behind it; Batty (2006) shows the distribution is stable while ranks churn.
- Pre-modern settlements follow the same scaling as modern cities (Ortman et al. 2014), and Reba et al. (2016) compile city populations from 3700 BCE to 2000 CE, which gives size distributions per era.
- Settlement area and infrastructure scale with population as N^(2/3)–N^(5/6) (Bettencourt et al. 2007; Ortman et al. 2014), which sets footprint sizes on zoomed maps.

**Numbers taken for task 8.4.** Clauset et al. (2009, Table 6.1): US city populations follow a power law with pdf exponent α = 2.37 above 52 460 people (rank-size exponent 1.37, p = 0.76). Rozenfeld et al. (2010), city clusters: US ζ = 0.97 ± 0.03 above 12 000 people (1947 clusters holding 171 of 271 million, 63%), Great Britain ζ = 1.07 ± 0.03 above 5000 (1007 clusters, 45 of 54.5 million, 83%); cluster area is close to proportional to population (slopes 0.96 and 1.07). Soo (2005, abstract): Zipf's law is rejected for 53 of 73 countries by OLS and 30 of 73 by the Hill estimator. Ortman et al. (2014): settled area scales as N^0.652 (95% CI 0.626–0.674) for settlements below 5000 and N^0.724 (0.434–1.135) above, against predictions of 2/3 and 5/6; their ~1500 settlements run from ~10 people to ~200 000. Reba et al. (2016): 1599 geolocated cities, 10 353 city–date populations, thresholds of 20 000–40 000 (Chandler) and 10 000 to 1 million by era (Modelski); Beijing ~1 million around 1800.

**Validation.** Maximum-likelihood exponent of generated settlements (Clauset et al. 2009) against Rozenfeld et al. (2010) for modern levels; the largest pre-industrial settlement against Reba et al. (2016); footprint area against population scaling on zoomed maps.

## 4. Placement

**Methods.**
- Weighted blue-noise sampling (Poisson disk, Bridson 2007) with an exclusion radius set by settlement size gives even spacing with hierarchy, as central-place theory expects.
- Procedural village generation on terrain uses the same suitability-then-seed approach at local scale (Emilien et al. 2012).
- A gravity or XTENT-like competition (Renfrew & Level 1979) can place higher-order centres first and let lower orders fill the gaps.

**Validation.** Nearest-neighbour spacing ratio by rank, the covariate shares of section 1, and the rank-size fit of section 3.

## 5. Roads and trade

**Methods.**
- Least-cost paths over a cost surface (Dijkstra 1959), with a slope cost from the hiking function of Tobler (Goodchild 2020) and river and wetland crossing costs. Galin et al. (2010) route roads over terrain this way with slope and curvature costs.
- The link set comes from a proximity graph over settlements: relative neighbourhood graph (Toussaint 1980), Gabriel graph (Gabriel & Sokal 1969), or a minimum spanning tree plus shortcuts. Strano et al. (2012) find that real networks grow by densification and exploration, and Barthélemy (2011) reviews their statistics (detour index, degree, face areas).

**Numbers taken for task 8.5.** Tobler's hiking function (Goodchild 2020, eq. 1): v = 6·e^(−3.5|g+0.05|) km/h, g the signed slope as rise over run, fastest (6 km/h) at g = −0.05. GRIP (Meijer et al. 2018): 21.6 million km of roads in 222 countries; lengths per class nearly double from highways to primary, secondary and tertiary roads; local roads are 23% of the total; per country, log10 length of main roads (highways and primary) = −5.23 + 1.01 log10 area + 0.80 log10 population density + 0.51 log10 GDP per capita + 0.36 OECD (all roads: 0.90, 0.52, 0.13, 0.36). Itiner-e (de Soto et al. 2025): 299 171 km of Roman roads over ~4 million km², 103 478 km main (34.6%) and 195 693 km secondary, 14 769 segments. Barthélemy (2011): street networks have average degree ≈ 2.5 and total length ≈ 1.51 N^0.49. Strano et al. (2012): a 125 km² area north of Milan grew from 255 nodes (1833) to over 5000 (2007), total length ∝ N^0.54, degree 2.4–2.8.

**Validation.** Grid road density in well-settled regions against Roman and GRIP main roads; route factor; degree; elasticity of main-road length to area and population density across settlement regions against Meijer et al. (2018).

## 6. Polities and borders

**Methods.**
- Territory from centres: XTENT, I = C^a − k·d, each cell goes to the centre with the highest influence (Renfrew & Level 1979), i.e. a weighted Voronoi diagram. On a cost surface instead of straight distance, borders fall on ridges and wide rivers.
- Rugged land fragments polities: in a grid model of polity competition, mountains and rivers make Europe split and China unify (Fernández-Villaverde et al. 2023). Turchin et al. (2013) model the spread of large states from military technology on a grid; that is milestone 9.
- Largest empire size grows logistically with era, with jumps at ~2800 BCE, ~600 BCE and ~1600 CE (Taagepera 1978), which bounds polity size by development level.

**Numbers taken for task 8.6.** Taagepera (1978, Table 2, Mm² of 133 Mm² of dry land without Antarctica): largest states 0.25 (Egypt, 2800 BCE), 5.5 (Achaemenid, 500 BCE), 6.2 (Han, 1 CE), 24 (Mongol, 1300), 12 (Russia, 1700), 30 (British, 1900), 34 (British, 1925), 22.5 (USSR, 1975); jumps around 2800 BCE, 600 BCE and 1600 CE. Taagepera (1997): the largest polity held 0.3–1% of dry land before 600 BCE, never below 2% after, rising in record from 5 to 20% until 1600 and 15–25% after; the effective number of polities by area, N_A = A²/Σ A_i², never fell below 8000 before 650 BCE, went from ~600 to 30 between 500 BCE and 1300 CE (back to ~1000 in breakdowns), stayed below 30 since 1800, reached 9 in 1925 and was 19 in 1990 and 24 in 1995; before states it may have been 10⁵–10⁶. Taagepera (1979) details growth and decline from 600 BCE to 600 CE.

**Validation.** Largest polity and effective number against Taagepera by communication level; share of border edges along rivers against Popelka & Smith (2020), 23% of national and 17% of first-order subnational borders, best measured on zoomed maps; straight-line borders only for colonial or treaty polities (Alesina et al. 2011).

**Result (task 8.10, zoomed maps).** On six Earth worlds, two regions each around towns on polity borders (electric culture, ~150 km at 0.75 km), a median 21% of border edges lie at lattice rivers of order 5 or more, against 0–4% of all land edges (Popelka & Smith: 23% national, 17% subnational; their river set differs, so this is indicative). Local roads reach 0.06–2.0 km/km² as density rises from 2 to 900 people/km²; GRIP's 21 million km give 0.16 km/km² over all land (Meijer et al. 2018). Built-up land covers 0–24% with Ortman et al. (2014) footprints and, from electric energy on, the densities of Angel et al. (2011, Table 3.3 and their regression on 3646 cities): overall density in cities above 100 000 was 60 people/ha in 2000 (world; 35 in developed and 88 in developing countries) at a median city of 209 468, 30 people/ha in cities of 10 000–100 000, and density rises 16% for each doubling of city population; average built-up density fell 1.5% a year in 30 cities from 1894 to 2000 and 2.0% a year in 120 cities from 1990 to 2000.

**Result (task 8.11).** `validate_culture.py` now gives each measure a verdict. The electric grid road density (0.015 km/km² against ~0.025) failed; modern cities take people from surrounding cells, so most populated cells had no road. With every cell of 500 people or more as a road node it is 0.022, and the main-road elasticities fell from 1.42 and 1.75 to 1.15 and 1.00 (Meijer: 1.01 and 0.80). The largest polity below Taagepera's remains a known gap.

## 7. Names

**Methods.**
- Per-culture phonology: a consonant and vowel inventory, syllable templates and phonotactic constraints, drawn from typological frequencies. WALS gives consonant inventory sizes (mean 22.7, median 21, 563 languages; Maddieson 2013a) and syllable structure (simple 61, moderately complex 274, complex 151 of 486 languages; Maddieson 2013b). PHOIBLE 2.0 gives 3020 phoneme inventories for segment frequencies (Moran et al. 2019).
- Word generation from syllable templates, with a small morphology of place-name elements (river, hill, fort, new) per culture; Hayes & Wilson (2008) give a maximum-entropy scoring of phonotactic well-formedness if templates alone read unnaturally.

**Numbers taken for task 8.7.** WALS (Maddieson 2013): consonant inventories in 563 languages, 6–14: 89, 15–18: 122, 19–25: 201, 26–33: 94, 34+: 57 (mean 22.7, median 21, range 6–122); vowel qualities in 564, 2–4: 93, 5–6: 287, 7–14: 184 (five vowels in 188); syllable structure in 486, simple 61, moderately complex 274, complex 151. PHOIBLE 2.0 (downloaded from the project's GitHub; 3020 inventories of 2175 languages, marginal segments left out): share of inventories with m 0.963, k 0.901, j 0.895, p 0.846, w 0.815, n 0.777, t 0.683, l 0.672, s 0.665, ŋ 0.623, b 0.623, ɡ 0.553, h 0.549; i 0.920, u 0.875, a 0.861, e 0.607, o 0.601; 37.1% of inventories have a long vowel.

**Validation.** Inventory sizes and syllable classes of generated cultures against WALS; how often each consonant appears against its PHOIBLE share; names unique within a culture and built from the culture's own syllables.

## 8. Hostile worlds and colonists

**Methods.**
- Landing sites: flat, low, safe terrain near targets of interest, with slope and rock limits and altitude for atmospheric braking (Golombek et al. 2012).
- Radiation: without a magnetic field or thick air the surface dose is high (Hassler et al. 2014 measured it on Mars), so shielded habitats (buried, underground) are preferred; Sauro et al. (2020) review the sizes of lava tubes on Earth, Moon and Mars as shelter sites.
- Orbital elevators need the synchronous orbit inside the Hill sphere and a spin period short enough (Pearson 1975); both follow from the existing orbit and bulk state.

**Fit to the model.** The state already has breathable air, magnetic field, surface pressure, temperature, gravity and occupiability; the surface has volcanic landforms and heat-pipe or stagnant-lid regimes that host lava tubes.

**Numbers taken for task 8.8.** Hassler et al. (2014): on the Martian surface (Gale crater, −4.4 km, ~21 g/cm² of air) the galactic cosmic-ray dose is 0.210 ± 0.040 mGy/day and the dose equivalent 0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day; Table 3 scales HZETRN models to these values: surface 76 mGy/yr and 232 mSv/yr; 10 cm (28 g/cm²) 96 and 295; 1 m (280 g/cm²) 36.4 and 81; 2 m (560 g/cm²) 8.7 and 15; 3 m (840 g/cm²) 1.8 and 2.9; below ~3 m cosmic rays no longer dominate. Sauro et al. (2020): Martian and lunar lava tubes are 1–3 orders of magnitude more voluminous than terrestrial ones, up to 80 times bigger on Mars. Pearson (1975): taper ratio of a balanced tower A_s/A_0 = exp[(r₀/h)(1 + r₀³/2r_s³ − 3r₀/2r_s)], e^(0.776 r₀/h) for Earth, with h = σ/ρg₀ the characteristic height; steel (h ≈ 50 km) needs e^99, graphite whiskers (h = 2150 km, σ = 46.5 GN/m²) a taper of 10; the balanced tower reaches ~150 000 km from Earth's centre. Golombek et al. (2012), MSL: latitude within ±30°, elevation below −1 km, relief under 100–130 m at 1–1000 m, slopes under 30° at 2–5 m, rock abundance under 8%.

## 9. Organic mode (milestone 9)

Researched 2026-09-27 before building. References were checked against NASA ADS (bibcode given) or, outside ADS, against the publisher's DOI page.

**Spread, numbers read (Pinhasi et al. 2005, supplied by Ben).** From 735 dated sites, the Neolithic spread over Europe ran at 0.6–1.3 km/yr (95% interval; 0.7–1.1 km/yr on great circles and 0.8–1.3 km/yr on shortest paths from the most likely origins, 0.6–1.0 and 0.7–1.1 with calibrated dates), with no overall acceleration or deceleration, starting 9000–10 500 uncalibrated or 10 000–11 500 calibrated years BP. The time-delayed demic model gives v = √(am/T) / (1 + aT/2) with a = 0.029–0.035 /yr (initial growth rate), m = 900–2200 km² per generation (mobility) and T = 29–35 yr (generation time), 0.6–1.1 km/yr (end points checked: 0.57 and 1.08 km/yr).

**Spread.** Population fronts that grow logistically and disperse randomly advance at a constant speed (Skellam 1951, following Fisher's wave of advance). Fort & Méndez (1999) add the delay of a generation between birth and dispersal and apply it to the Neolithic transition in Europe; Fort (2012) combines demic diffusion (farmers moving) with cultural diffusion (foragers adopting); Davison et al. (2006) model faster spread along waterways. Pinhasi et al. (2005) date the spread from radiocarbon sites. The numbers of the other papers are *to extract* when task 9.3 calibrates the dispersal.

**Farming origins and population.** Kavanagh et al. (2018) hindcast forager densities to study where farming began; Bocquet-Appel (2011) describes the Neolithic demographic transition; Bevan et al. (2017) link Holocene population swings to food production and climate. HYDE 3.2 (section 1) and the KK10 land-use reconstruction (Kaplan et al. 2011) give population and land use through the Holocene.

**Turchin et al. (2013), read (supplied by Ben).** Afro-Eurasia on 100 × 100 km cells, 1500 BCE–1500 CE, yearly steps; only agricultural cells are modelled, each a community with binary ultrasociality (U) and military-technology (M) traits, and every polity starts as one cell. Every border cell gets one chance a year to attack a random neighbour (four land neighbours; coastal cells can attack other coastal cells within a sea distance), so attack rates scale with border length. Attacker power 1 + βU·S, defender power 1 + βU·S + γE (E the defending cell's elevation in km, since mountains are easier to defend); success probability (P_att − P_def)/(P_att + P_def), no success when negative; a won cell is annexed. Military technology starts on the steppe edge and spreads to a random neighbour with probability σ, never lost. Ultrasocial traits appear and vanish by cultural shift (μ01 ≪ μ10) and are copied on annexation (ethnocide) with a probability rising with the attacker's military technology and falling with elevation. Each polity dissolves into its cells each year with probability δ₀ + δ_S·S − δ_a·U. With 12 parameters (four influential) the model explains about two thirds of the variance in imperial density over 7941 data points (2647 agricultural cells × 3 eras), R² ~0.5 or better over a broad range of parameters; turning off elevation lowers the fit. There is no distance decay of power from a capital (an earlier message of this session said there was; that was wrong).

**Polities (task 9.5).** The model follows Turchin et al. (2013) in its attack rule, power 1 + β·S, elevation term and break-up rate δ₀ + δ_S·S − δ_c·c, with S in their 100 km cells. Its departures: power decays from the capital with a reach set by communication; a continuous cohesion replaces the binary ultrasocial trait; military technology is not modelled (the reach does part of its work); a break-up leaves the capital's core as an heir. Targets: Taagepera's largest polity and effective number by communication level (milestone 8) and Arbesman's (2011) mean empire lifespan of about 220 years. Results in DESIGN.md 4.8.

**Rulers, wars and alliances (task 9.6), read.** Kokkonen & Sundell (2012, QoG working paper 2012:3, published in APSR 2014): 960 monarchs of 42 European states, 1000–1800; mean tenure 20.9 years with primogeniture and 11.4 without; 16% against over 50% deposed; primogeniture lowers the hazard of deposition by 75%; four in ten monarchs were deposed or died in civil war in the first two centuries, fewer than one in five by the end. Kokkonen & Sundell (2020): successions raised the risk of civil war in 28 European states over 800 years; primogeniture mitigated it. Blaydes & Chaney (2013): mean ruler duration 11–15 years in the Muslim world 700–1500 (3047 rulers), with Western European rulers similar until about 1100 and significantly longer after. Gavrilets, Anderson & Turchin (2010): chiefdoms of villages on a hexagonal grid; the stronger attacks the weakest neighbour with success Fᵢ^α/(Fᵢ^α + Fⱼ^α); a chief's tenure τ = 5–20 years; on a chief's death a random number of subordinates become independent without war; subordinates secede if they expect to repel their master; complex chiefdoms live under 55–68 years; α and τ dominate the outcomes. Clauset (2018): 95 interstate wars of the Correlates of War data, 1823–2003; severity follows a power law with α = 1.53 ± 0.07 above 7061 battle deaths (the upper 54%); one onset every 1.91 years on average. Cederman (2003, supplied by Ben): COW interstate wars 1820–1997 have a cumulative severity distribution with slope −0.41 (R² 0.985), Levy's great-power wars 1495–1965 −0.57; his model (≈200 states on a 50 × 50 grid) has states at peace attack with probability 0.01 per period, on alert (a neighbour fights) every period, when the local power ratio passes a logistic threshold at 3:1 (slope 20); alert ends at 0.1 per period; a campaign is dropped with probability 0.2 per battle; power projection falls with distance from the capital; war clusters join adjacent fighting states, with severity the summed battle damage (10% of the resources on a front). Base runs give slopes −0.49 to −0.64 (median −0.55); without technological change or context activation the power law is lost. The model of task 9.6 takes Cederman's onset threshold, alert, dropped campaigns and clusters, Kokkonen & Sundell's reigns and primogeniture effect, and Gavrilets et al.'s break-up at successions; alliances are heuristic (Cederman leaves them out). Results in DESIGN.md 4.8.

**Settlements, roads and trade through time (task 9.7), read.** Bosker & Buringh (2017): 1784 potential city sites in Europe west of the Trieste–St Petersburg line, 800–1800, cities of at least 5000 people; 34 cities in 800 and over 1450 in 1800, urbanisation from 3% to 15% and urban population from 0.7 to 21 million; sites on navigable rivers had about 6 percentage points more chance of becoming a city, non-hub places on Roman roads less, and sites 20–100 km from an existing city more than nearer or farther ones; early geographic advantages persisted. Dalgaard et al. (2018): across the Roman Empire of 117 CE, denser Roman roads go with denser modern roads, more settlement in 500 CE and more economic activity in 2010; the persistence is lost where wheeled transport was abandoned. Barjamovic et al. (2019): Old Assyrian merchants' records give a distance elasticity of trade of 1.9 (s.e. 0.19) in the Bronze Age; log ancient city size rises with log natural-road connectivity (coefficient 1.4). Disdier & Head (2008): 1467 distance elasticities from 103 papers have mean 0.91 and median 0.87 (5th–95th percentiles 0.28–1.55); the effect fell slightly from 1870 to 1950 and rose after. Reba et al. (2016): a geolocated dataset of 1741 cities and 10 353 city–date populations, 3700 BC–AD 2000, from Chandler (1987) and Modelski (2003), with thresholds that change by era. Task 9.7 takes the distance elasticities and the persistence of roads and sites; results in DESIGN.md 4.8.

**Colonists and natives (task 9.8), read.** Koch et al. (2019): the Americas held 60.5 million people before 1492 (IQR 44.8–78.2 million); European epidemics (smallpox, measles, influenza, plague), with warfare, exploitation and famine, removed 90% (IQR 87–92%) over the next century, 54.5 million deaths; 55.8 Mha of farmland was abandoned and regrew. Bazzi, Fiszbein & Gebresilasse (2017, NBER w23997): the US frontier line is where density falls below two people per square mile; from 1790 to 1890 the US population rose from 3.9 to 62.6 million and its settled area from under 240 000 to nearly 2 000 000 square miles; counties spent a mean of 18 years (s.d. 11) in the frontier belt. Moreau et al. (2011, abstract only) find a reproductive advantage at the front of the Quebec colonisation wave. Abrams & Strogatz (2003, read, supplied by Ben): speakers convert from Y to X at P_yx = c·x^a·s and back at P_xy = c·(1 − x)^a·(1 − s), so dx/dt = y·P_yx − x·P_xy; only x = 0 and x = 1 are stable, so two languages cannot coexist; fitted to 42 regions of Peru, Scotland, Wales, Bolivia, Ireland and Alsace-Lorraine, a = 1.31 ± 0.25; fitted statuses of the declining languages 0.33 (Scottish Gaelic, Sutherland), 0.26 (Quechua, Huanuco), 0.40 (Welsh, Monmouthshire), 0.43 and 0.46 (Welsh, all Wales); c is not printed (fitting their curves as read from Fig. 1 gives 0.084–0.089 per year). Comin & Hobijn (2010, read): 15 technologies in 166 countries, 1820–2003; mean adoption lag 45 years (s.d. 39); technologies invented ten years later are adopted 4.3 years faster; steam ships ~120 years, passenger railways ~97, telegraph ~46, electricity ~56, cars ~44, personal computers ~14; lag differences account for at least a quarter of income differences. Results in DESIGN.md 4.8.

**Colonial dominance, independence, bilingualism and creoles (task 9.8, follow-up 2), read.** Strang (1991, supplied by Ben): 165 transitions from dependency to sovereignty 1500–1987 among 478 Western dependencies; the rate is virtually zero until the late 18th century, rises sharply but briefly with the American wars of independence (1776–1830, when the largest settler colonies broke away), stays near zero 1831–1923 (one event), then 130 dependencies become sovereign in seventy years (18 African ones in 1960, 25 microstates in the 1970s). The integrated hazard is linear in the dependency's age, so the rate does not depend on age or on time since the present administration; historical time and external conditions drive it. **Farming and development (task 9.4), read (supplied by Ben).** Larson et al. (2014): food production began globally 12 000–11 000 years ago; at least 11 regions of the Old and New World were independent centres of origin, with several more suggested; the two periods of greatest interest are 12 000–9000 BP and 7000–4000 BP (their Fig. 1 marks 12 000–8200 and 8200–4200 BP). (2018): environmental, geographic and cultural variables capture 77% of the variation in population density among 220 foraging societies; hindcast potential densities rose with improving conditions during the periods when domestication arose in every known origin centre (12 in their figure), supporting the surplus hypothesis. Kremer (1993): output Y = A p^α T^(1−α), population adjusts to the income it can sustain, and technology grows at Ȧ/A = g p (each person equally likely to invent), so population growth is proportional to population; Jones's general research equation Ȧ = g p A^φ weakens the feedback for φ < 1; among regions without contact after the land bridges flooded (Old World, Americas, mainland Australia, Tasmania, Flinders Island) those with larger initial populations had faster technological change by 1500. **Suitability (task 9.2).** Zabel et al. (2014, read) rate agricultural suitability by fuzzy membership functions for climate (temperature and precipitation over each crop's growing cycle), soil (texture, coarse fragments, gypsum, base saturation, pH, organic carbon, salinity, sodicity) and slope; the lowest membership decides. Slope must not exceed 16% for most crops (not for oil palm and paddy rice); growth needs temperatures above 5 °C (1 °C for winter crops), 20 mm of rain in the first two weeks, a mean annual temperature above 0 °C (no permafrost) and mean daily radiation above 60 W/m². For 1981–2010, 77.7 of 127.5 million km² are suitable for rainfed crops: 5.8 highly, 41.3 moderately and 30.6 marginally. Nunn & Puga (2012, definition read from the NBER working paper) define the terrain ruggedness index as √Σ(e_neighbour − e_centre)² over the eight neighbours of a 30″ grid, in hundreds of metres: Netherlands 0.037, Mauritania 0.115, Zimbabwe 1.194, Romania 1.267, Nepal 5.043, Lesotho 6.202, African mean 1.110; rugged ground protected people from the slave trades, which gave ruggedness an indirect positive effect on income in Africa.
